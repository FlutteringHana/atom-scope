## 1. Purpose and Scope

### 1.1 Purpose

This document defines the physics knowledge architecture of AtomScope.

The purpose is to establish a structured understanding of the core physical theories, models, mathematical relationships, and experimental phenomena covered in the Atom section of the Atom and Photons / Quantum Optics course.

Rather than reproducing the original lecture sequence or presenting a chronological history of atomic physics, this architecture reorganizes the subject around **Physics Core Knowledge**.

The primary focus is on:

- Understanding fundamental physical concepts and principles.
    
- Understanding atomic models and their physical assumptions.
    
- Exploring mathematical descriptions and relationships.
    
- Understanding physical phenomena predicted or explained by these models.
    
- Connecting theoretical knowledge with experimental evidence.
    
- Recognizing the applicability and limitations of different models.
    

Historical context is retained as supplementary material to explain the motivation, significance, and development of physical theories.

### 1.2 Scope

The current knowledge architecture covers three major areas:

1. Atomic Structure and Nuclear Model
    
2. Quantization and Atomic Energy States
    
3. Introduction to Quantum Mechanics
    

The scope begins with the discovery of electrons and the development of early atomic models, proceeds through energy quantization and atomic energy states, and concludes with the fundamental mathematical framework of quantum mechanics.

The quantum mechanics section includes:

- Wave-particle duality.
    
- De Broglie matter waves.
    
- Schrödinger equation.
    
- Wave functions and probability interpretation.
    
- Quantum states and atomic orbitals.
    

Historical extensions of early quantum theory, including the Bohr-Sommerfeld model, are also included because of their theoretical significance and their role in the development of atomic physics.

Advanced topics such as spin-orbit coupling, angular momentum coupling, perturbation theory, and advanced quantum optical interactions remain outside the initial scope unless subsequently required.

## 2. Knowledge Organization Philosophy

### 2.1 Theory-Centered Organization

The knowledge structure is organized around physical theories, models, and principles rather than historical events.

The central learning question is:

**What physical knowledge do we need to understand, and how are these concepts connected?**

Historical events and experiments provide context and evidence, but they do not determine the primary organization of the knowledge structure.

### 2.2 Four Complementary Dimensions

The architecture follows four complementary dimensions.

|Dimension|Purpose|Examples|
|---|---|---|
|Physical Theory and Models|Describe physical systems and establish theoretical frameworks|Rutherford Model, Bohr Model|
|Mathematical Description|Express physical principles quantitatively|Energy Levels, Rydberg Formula|
|Physical Phenomena and Experiments|Demonstrate and investigate physical behavior|Photoelectric Effect, Atomic Spectroscopy|
|Historical Context|Explain the motivation and development of theories|Rutherford's Model, Planck's Quantum Hypothesis|

These dimensions are related but should not be treated as equivalent categories.

For example, the Bohr Model is a physical model, its energy equation is a mathematical description, hydrogen emission lines are an observable phenomenon, and the historical development of the model provides context.

### 2.3 Model-Oriented Learning Framework

Each major physical model or theoretical concept should be studied through the following questions:

1. **What is it?**  
    Define the physical model, theory, or principle.
    
2. **How does it work?**  
    Explain its assumptions, physical mechanisms, and mathematical relationships.
    
3. **What does it predict?**  
    Identify the physical phenomena and observable results that follow from the model.
    
4. **How do we verify it?**  
    Examine the relevant experimental evidence.
    
5. **What are its limitations?**  
    Identify the conditions under which the model is valid and where it becomes insufficient.
    

This framework provides a consistent structure for learning materials and future visualization modules.

## Part II — Quantization and Atomic Energy States

**Central Question:** Why does an atom possess discrete energy states, and how do electrons transition between them?

This part introduces quantum ideas and applies energy quantization to atomic structure.

It is the central theoretical component of the Atom section.

The knowledge is organized into three interconnected areas.

### 4.1 Foundations of Quantization

This section introduces the physical phenomena and theoretical ideas that motivate the transition from classical physics to quantum physics.

#### 4.1.1 Blackbody Radiation

Core concepts:

- Blackbody and thermal radiation.
    
- Spectral energy distribution.
    
- Classical Rayleigh-Jeans Law.
    
- Ultraviolet Catastrophe.
    
- Planck's Quantum Hypothesis.
    
- Energy quantization.
    

Key mathematical relationship:

E = nhf

Where:

- E represents the quantized energy.
    
- n is an integer.
    
- h is Planck's constant.
    
- f is the radiation frequency.
    

The central idea is that Planck introduced energy quantization to explain the observed blackbody radiation spectrum.

#### 4.1.2 Photoelectric Effect

Core concepts:

- Photon concept.
    
- Photon energy.
    
- Work function.
    
- Threshold frequency.
    
- Maximum kinetic energy of photoelectrons.
    
- Relationship between light intensity and frequency.
    

Key mathematical relationships:

E_photon = hf

K_max = hf - φ

Where φ represents the work function of the material.

The photoelectric effect demonstrates the particle-like behavior of light and establishes the relationship between photon energy and frequency.

### 4.2 Atomic Energy Models

This section applies quantization ideas to atomic structure.

#### 4.2.1 Bohr Model

**Primary Model of Part II.**

Core concepts:

- Rutherford nuclear model as the starting point.
    
- Bohr's postulates.
    
- Stationary electron orbits.
    
- Angular momentum quantization.
    
- Quantized orbital radii.
    
- Quantized atomic energy levels.
    
- Ground state and excited states.
    
- Atomic stability within the Bohr model.
    

Key mathematical relationships:

Angular momentum quantization:

L = mvr = nℏ

Hydrogen energy levels:

E_n = -13.6/n² eV

Bohr radius:

r_n = a₀n²

The Bohr model introduces discrete electron states and provides an early theoretical explanation for atomic stability and hydrogen's spectral lines.

#### 4.2.2 Generalizations and Extensions of the Bohr Model

The Bohr model was an important step toward understanding atomic energy quantization. However, its original formulation was limited in scope.

Two important extensions should be considered: the generalization to hydrogen-like atoms and ions, and the development of the Bohr-Sommerfeld model.

##### A. Hydrogen-like Atoms and Ions

Core concepts:

- Nuclear charge Z.
    
- Single-electron atomic systems.
    
- Hydrogen-like ions.
    
- Energy-level dependence on nuclear charge.
    
- Orbital radius dependence on nuclear charge.
    
- Energy transitions in hydrogen-like systems.
    

A hydrogen-like atom or ion consists of a single electron interacting with a nucleus of charge +Ze.

Examples:

- Hydrogen: H
    
- Singly ionized helium: He⁺
    
- Doubly ionized lithium: Li²⁺
    

For a hydrogen-like system, the Bohr energy levels are:

E_n = -13.6Z²/n² eV

The corresponding orbital radii are:

r_n = a₀n²/Z

Where:

- Z is the nuclear charge number.
    
- n is the principal quantum number.
    
- a₀ is the Bohr radius.
    

These relationships generalize the hydrogen atom model to other one-electron systems.

The generalization demonstrates how atomic energy levels and characteristic radii depend on nuclear charge.

##### B. Bohr-Sommerfeld Model

The Bohr-Sommerfeld model, also known as the Sommerfeld extension of the Bohr model, represents an important development of the old quantum theory.

It extends the original Bohr model by introducing additional quantization conditions and allowing more general electron trajectories.

Core concepts:

- Circular and elliptical electron orbits.
    
- Additional quantum numbers.
    
- Quantization of radial and angular motion.
    
- Action quantization conditions.
    
- Relativistic corrections.
    
- Fine structure of hydrogen spectral lines.
    

In the old quantum theory, the motion of a particle was subject to action quantization conditions:

∮ p_i dq_i = n_i h

For a central-force system, the angular motion and radial motion can be quantized separately.

This extends the original Bohr model, which only considered circular electron orbits.

Sommerfeld also introduced relativistic corrections to the electron motion, providing an early theoretical explanation for aspects of the fine structure of hydrogen spectral lines.

##### C. Physical Significance and Limitations

The Bohr-Sommerfeld model represents an important historical and theoretical transition between the original Bohr model and modern quantum mechanics.

Its significance includes:

- Extending the mathematical framework of the original Bohr model.
    
- Introducing additional quantization conditions.
    
- Improving the description of atomic spectral structure.
    
- Providing an important intermediate step toward modern quantum theory.
    

However, the old quantum theory remained incomplete.

Its orbit-based description could not consistently explain all atomic phenomena and was eventually superseded by modern quantum mechanics.

**Primary Learning Focus:**

Understand both the generalization of the Bohr model to hydrogen-like systems and the theoretical significance of the Bohr-Sommerfeld extension.

These are distinct developments and should be studied separately.

For hydrogen-like ions:

E_n = -13.6Z²/n² eV

r_n = a₀n²/Z

Examples:

- Hydrogen: H
    
- Singly ionized helium: He⁺
    
- Doubly ionized lithium: Li²⁺
    

**Course-specific clarification required:** The term "Generalization of the Bohr Model" may also refer to the Bohr-Sommerfeld extension or other treatments in the course material. Its exact scope and mathematical content should be confirmed against the lecture notes before finalizing the corresponding learning module.

## Part III — Introduction to Quantum Mechanics

**Central Question:** How should microscopic particles be described beyond classical particle models?

This part introduces the conceptual transition from classical atomic models to quantum mechanical descriptions.

### 5.1 Wave-Particle Duality

Core concepts:

- Classical particle and wave descriptions.
    
- Wave properties of light.
    
- Particle properties of light.
    
- Wave-particle duality.
    
- Complementary descriptions of microscopic phenomena.
    

The central idea is that microscopic objects cannot always be adequately described using purely classical particle or wave models.

### 5.2 De Broglie Matter Waves

Core concepts:

- Matter-wave hypothesis.
    
- Particle momentum and wavelength.
    
- De Broglie wavelength.
    
- Electron diffraction.
    
- Matter-wave interference.
    
- Standing matter waves.
    

Key mathematical relationship:

λ = h/p

For non-relativistic particles:

λ = h/mv

The de Broglie hypothesis extends wave-particle duality to matter.

#### Connection to the Bohr Model

For an electron moving around a circular orbit, the standing-wave condition can be expressed as:

2πr = nλ

Combining this with the de Broglie wavelength gives:

mvr = nℏ

This provides a wave-based interpretation of the angular momentum quantization condition in the Bohr model.

This relationship is an important conceptual bridge between early atomic models and quantum mechanics.

### 5.3 Wave Function

Core concepts:

- Quantum state description.
    
- Wave function ψ.
    
- Complex-valued wave functions.
    
- Probability amplitude.
    
- Probability density.
    
- Born interpretation.
    
- Normalization.
    

Key mathematical relationship:

Probability density:

ρ = |ψ|²

Normalization:

∫ |ψ|² dV = 1

The wave function provides a mathematical description of a quantum state.

Its squared magnitude represents the probability density of finding a particle in a particular region of space.

This replaces the idea of a precisely defined classical electron trajectory with a probabilistic description.

### 5.4 Schrödinger Equation

The Schrödinger equation provides the fundamental mathematical framework for describing quantum states and their evolution.

It establishes the relationship between a quantum system's wave function, energy, and Hamiltonian operator.

Core concepts:

- Quantum state evolution.
    
- Hamiltonian operator.
    
- Time-dependent Schrödinger equation.
    
- Time-independent Schrödinger equation.
    
- Energy eigenstates and eigenvalues.
    
- Stationary states.
    
- Quantized energy solutions.
    

#### Time-dependent Schrödinger Equation

iℏ ∂ψ/∂t = Ĥψ

This equation describes the time evolution of a quantum state.

#### Time-independent Schrödinger Equation

Ĥψ = Eψ

This eigenvalue equation is used to determine stationary quantum states and their corresponding energy eigenvalues for time-independent Hamiltonians.

For a particle in a potential V, the Hamiltonian in the non-relativistic formulation is:

Ĥ = -ℏ²/(2m) ∇² + V

The Schrödinger equation provides a mathematical basis for understanding why quantum systems can possess discrete energy eigenvalues.

#### Connection to Atomic Structure

For hydrogen and hydrogen-like atoms, solving the Schrödinger equation produces:

- Allowed atomic energy eigenvalues.
    
- Corresponding wave functions.
    
- Spatial probability distributions.
    
- Quantum states characterized by quantum numbers.
    

This provides a more complete quantum mechanical description of atomic structure than the Bohr model.

**Primary Learning Focus:**

Understand the physical meaning and mathematical role of the Schrödinger equation, and how it connects wave functions with atomic energy states.

### 5.5 Quantum States and Atomic Orbitals

This section develops the quantum mechanical description of electrons in atoms.

Unlike the Bohr model, modern quantum mechanics does not describe electrons as particles moving along well-defined classical orbits.

Instead, electrons are described by quantum states represented by wave functions.

#### 5.5.1 Quantum States

Core concepts:

- Quantum states.
    
- Energy eigenstates.
    
- Stationary states.
    
- Quantum numbers.
    
- State-dependent physical properties.
    
- Superposition of quantum states (if covered).
    

For hydrogen-like atoms, quantum states can be characterized by quantum numbers associated with the solutions of the Schrödinger equation.

The principal quantum number n determines the energy level in the non-relativistic Coulomb model.

The orbital angular momentum quantum number l and magnetic quantum number m_l describe additional properties of the spatial state.

#### 5.5.2 Atomic Orbitals

Core concepts:

- Atomic wave functions.
    
- Spatial probability distributions.
    
- Probability density.
    
- Orbital shapes.
    
- Radial and angular components.
    
- Nodes and nodal surfaces.
    
- s, p, and d orbitals.
    

An atomic orbital is associated with a quantum state of an electron in an atom.

The probability density is given by:

ρ(r) = |ψ(r)|²

It describes the probability of finding an electron per unit volume at a particular position.

Different quantum states produce different spatial probability distributions.

For example:

- 1s orbital.
    
- 2s orbital.
    
- 2p orbitals.
    
- Higher-energy atomic orbitals.
    

The spatial structures of these orbitals reflect the mathematical properties of their wave functions.

#### 5.5.3 Bohr Orbits vs. Quantum Orbitals

An important conceptual distinction must be established.

|Bohr Orbit|Quantum Orbital|
|---|---|
|Classical-like electron trajectory|Quantum state described by a wave function|
|Definite orbital radius|Spatial probability distribution|
|Electron position follows an assumed orbit|Position is described probabilistically|
|Early quantum model|Modern quantum mechanical description|

Atomic orbitals should not be interpreted as literal paths traveled by electrons.

They represent quantum states and their associated spatial probability distributions.

#### 5.5.4 Physical Significance

Atomic orbitals provide the foundation for understanding:

- The spatial structure of atoms.
    
- Electron probability distributions.
    
- Atomic energy states.
    
- The organization of electrons in atoms.
    
- The relationship between quantum mechanics and chemical structure.
    

**Primary Learning Focus:**

Understand how wave functions describe atomic quantum states, how probability density is obtained, and why modern atomic orbitals are fundamentally different from Bohr's classical-like electron orbits.

### 5.6 Experimental Foundation

The wave-like behavior of matter and the quantum mechanical description of microscopic particles are supported by experimental observations.

**Davisson-Germer Experiment**

Demonstrates electron diffraction and provides experimental evidence for the wave-like properties of electrons.

**Electron Diffraction**

Provides experimental evidence for matter-wave behavior and connects the de Broglie hypothesis with observable interference and diffraction phenomena.

### 5.7 Learning Outcomes

Learners should be able to:

- Explain wave-particle duality.
    
- Calculate the de Broglie wavelength of a particle.
    
- Understand the physical meaning of matter waves.
    
- Explain the relationship between standing waves and quantized states.
    
- Understand the physical meaning of the wave function.
    
- Interpret probability density and normalization.
    
- Understand the role of the Schrödinger equation in determining quantum states.
    
- Distinguish energy eigenstates from arbitrary quantum states.
    
- Understand the basic quantum numbers associated with atomic states.
    
- Interpret the spatial probability distributions of atomic orbitals.
    
- Distinguish Bohr orbits from modern quantum orbitals.

# 6. Cross-Part Conceptual Relationships

The three parts should not be understood as independent chapters.

They form a connected development of physical ideas.

### 6.1 Atomic Structure → Quantization

Rutherford's model establishes the nuclear structure of atoms but cannot explain atomic stability within classical electromagnetic theory.

This motivates the introduction of quantization and the Bohr model.

### 6.2 Quantum Origins → Atomic Energy States

Planck's quantization hypothesis and Einstein's photon model establish important quantum concepts.

Bohr applies quantization to atomic structure and introduces discrete atomic energy levels.

### 6.3 Atomic Energy States → Experimental Spectroscopy

Discrete atomic energy differences lead to specific photon energies and wavelengths.

These predictions can be investigated through atomic spectroscopy.

Franck-Hertz experiments provide complementary evidence through electron-atom collisions.

### 6.4 Bohr Model → Bohr-Sommerfeld Model → Modern Quantum Mechanics

The original Bohr model introduced angular momentum quantization and discrete atomic energy levels.

The Bohr-Sommerfeld model extended this framework through additional quantization conditions, more general orbital trajectories, and relativistic corrections.

Although these extensions improved the description of certain atomic spectral features, the old quantum theory remained incomplete.

Modern quantum mechanics subsequently replaced the orbit-based description with a mathematical framework based on wave functions and quantum states.

This progression illustrates the development of increasingly sophisticated descriptions of atomic structure.

### 6.5 Matter Waves → Schrödinger Equation → Atomic Orbitals

The de Broglie hypothesis establishes the wave-like properties of matter.

The Schrödinger equation provides the mathematical framework for describing these matter waves as quantum states.

Its solutions determine the allowed energy eigenvalues and corresponding wave functions.

For atomic systems, these wave functions produce spatial probability distributions known as atomic orbitals.

This creates the conceptual transition from classical-like electron trajectories to the probabilistic description of electrons in modern quantum mechanics.

# 9. Physics Accuracy and Scope Principles

All future learning materials and visualization modules should follow these principles.

1. **Physical correctness comes first.** Visual appearance must not compromise physical accuracy.
    
2. **Distinguish models from reality.** A model is a theoretical description with assumptions and limitations, not necessarily a literal representation of microscopic reality.
    
3. **Make approximations explicit.** Simplifications used in simulations must be documented.
    
4. **Connect mathematics with physical meaning.** Equations should be accompanied by explanations of their variables, assumptions, and observable implications.
    
5. **Distinguish classical and quantum descriptions.** In particular, Bohr orbits must not be presented as equivalent to modern quantum orbitals.
    
6. **Use experiments as evidence.** Experimental visualizations should demonstrate what is actually measured and how the results relate to theoretical predictions.
    
7. **Preserve historical context without making it the primary learning structure.** Historical information should explain theoretical motivation and scientific significance.
    
8. **Follow course-specific scope.** Topics that go beyond the actual course coverage should be clearly identified as extensions rather than mandatory course content.

## 11. Final Architecture Summary

AtomScope organizes its initial Atomic Physics learning content into three major parts:

**Part I — Atomic Structure and Nuclear Model**

Understand the internal structure of atoms through electron discovery and the Rutherford nuclear model.

**Part II — Quantization and Atomic Energy States**

Understand energy quantization, the Bohr model, atomic energy levels, transitions, and their experimental manifestations.

**Part III — Introduction to Quantum Mechanics**

Understand wave-particle duality, matter waves, and the transition toward wave-function-based descriptions of microscopic particles.

The overall learning progression is:

**Atomic Structure → Quantization → Atomic Energy States → Matter Waves → Quantum States**

The guiding principle is:

**Theory-centered, Model-driven, Experiment-supported, Historically-informed.**

This architecture serves as the physics knowledge foundation of AtomScope.
